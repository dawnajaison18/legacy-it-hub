Part 1 — The basics

No jargon. Read these six in order and networking starts to click.

01

What a network actually is

A network is just devices that can talk to each other — computers, phones, printers, servers. The internet is simply a huge number of these networks joined together. That's the whole idea. Everything else is detail about how the talking happens.

Think of it like the postal system. Every device is a building with a street address. When one device sends information, it's like mailing a stack of labelled envelopes — each has a "to" address and a "from" address — and machines in the middle (think post offices) pass them along until they arrive. Networking is mostly just: what's the address, and how does the envelope get there?
Those "envelopes" are called packets. A big file (say a photo) is chopped into many small packets, sent across the network, and reassembled at the other end. If a few get lost, they're re-sent.
02

The words you'll hear every day

Six terms cover most everyday conversations. Learn these and most things make sense.

IP address

Your device's address on the network, like 192.168.1.50. Like a street address — it tells traffic where to go. Office networks reuse the same "private" ranges (192.168.x.x, 10.x.x.x) inside the building.

MAC address

A device's permanent serial number, baked into the hardware, like 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E. Like a car's VIN — it never changes, even when the IP address does.

Default gateway

The way out of your local network — almost always the router. Like the front door of your building: anything not inside goes out through it.

DHCP

The automatic address clerk. When a device joins, DHCP hands it an IP address so nobody sets one by hand. When DHCP fails, devices get no internet — a very common problem.

DNS

The phone book of the internet. It turns names you remember (google.com) into the number addresses devices actually use. Site won't open by name but works by number? Suspect DNS.

Subnet

Your neighborhood — a group of addresses that can talk directly. Same subnet = they chat directly; anything outside goes out through the gateway.

One mental model to keep: a switch and router move traffic, a firewall judges it, DHCP assigns addresses, and DNS translates names. Keep those four verbs straight and most setups make sense.
03

The boxes on the wall

The physical equipment, and what each one really does — in plain terms.

Modem

The translator between your building and your internet provider. It converts the provider's signal into something your network understands. Usually one per site.

Router

The traffic director (the post office). It decides where data goes and connects your local network to the internet. Your "gateway" is the router.

Switch

A smart splitter. Connects many wired devices inside the building and sends each bit of traffic only to the device it's meant for.

Access Point (AP)

The thing that broadcasts Wi-Fi. It adds wireless to a wired network so phones and laptops can join without a cable.

Firewall

The security guard. It checks traffic against a rulebook and allows or blocks it. Often built into the router.

Hub (old)

An ancient "dumb switch" that shouted every message to every device. Replaced by switches — you'll rarely see one.

A small office often has one combined box (modem + router + Wi-Fi + firewall). A clinic usually has them as separate devices so each can be sized and secured properly.
04

What happens when you open a website

Here's the whole trip when you type an address and press enter — it ties every term above together:

1

Name → number (DNS). Your PC asks DNS, "what's the address for google.com?" and gets back a number (an IP address).

2

Local or far away? Your PC checks whether that address is in its own neighborhood (subnet). It isn't — so the request is handed to the default gateway (the router).

3

Out through the router. The router forwards the request toward the internet, through the modem and your provider.

4

The reply comes back the same way. The firewall lets the expected answer back in, and the page loads — all in a fraction of a second.

Notice every key term appeared once: DNS (name→number), subnet (local or not), gateway/router (the way out), firewall (lets the reply back). That's the whole game.
05

Wi-Fi made simple

Two practical things matter: the name and password people connect to, and which band they're on.

SSID

The network's name — the thing you pick from the Wi-Fi list. "SSID" is just the technical word for it.

Password & security

Wi-Fi is locked with WPA2 or WPA3 encryption. Rule of thumb: use WPA3 if all devices support it, WPA2 otherwise. Never use the old WEP/WPA — they're broken.

Guest network

A separate Wi-Fi name for visitors that keeps them off the internal systems. Always use one in a clinic.

Channel

A specific "lane" within a band. When nearby networks crowd the same lane, everyone slows down — changing channel can fix slow Wi-Fi.

The two bands (lanes) Wi-Fi uses

BandReachSpeedIn plain words
2.4 GHzFartherSlowerReaches through walls, but slower and crowded. Good for far-away devices.
5 GHzShorterFasterThe everyday choice when you're near the access point.
6 GHzShortestFastestNewest (Wi-Fi 6E/7). Very fast and clean, but needs modern hardware.
Slow Wi-Fi is usually distance, walls, too many devices, or a crowded channel — not a broken network. Moving closer or switching to 5 GHz fixes most of it.
06

Cables made simple

Wired connections are still the most reliable. The everyday cable is Ethernet, ending in a clip-in plug called RJ45 (looks like a fat phone connector).

The cable

An Ethernet cable clicks into the port. One run can be up to 100 metres (≈330 ft) before the signal weakens.

Categories

Higher number = faster. Cat5e (1 Gbps, the common baseline) and Cat6 / Cat6a (up to 10 Gbps). For a normal desk, Cat5e or Cat6 is plenty.

PoE (Power over Ethernet)

Sends power AND data down one cable — so a Wi-Fi access point, IP phone, or camera needs no separate power adapter.

Fiber optic

Glass cable that carries data as light. Very fast, immune to electrical interference — used for long runs and between buildings.

Handy tip: the small link light next to a network port tells you a lot. No light usually means a dead cable, a bad port, or nothing connected at the other end.
Part 2 — Fixing problems

This is the part of the job you'll actually do at a desk.

07

Address types & the big red flag

Private vs public

Private addresses (192.168.x.x, 10.x.x.x) work only inside your building. Public addresses work on the open internet and come from your provider. Many private devices share one public address to get out — that sharing is called NAT.

Static vs dynamic

Dynamic = handed out automatically by DHCP, can change. Static = set by hand and never changes — used for printers, servers, and network gear so they're always findable.

169.254.x.x — the red flag

If a device shows an address starting 169.254, it means it never got a real address (DHCP failed). One of the most common "no internet" causes. Fix: ipconfig /release then /renew.

127.0.0.1 — "myself"

Always means "this device itself." Handy for checking that the computer's own networking is alive.

You'll also hear IPv6 — a newer, much longer address format (like 2001:db8::1) created because the world ran out of the older IPv4 numbers. For most desk work, treat it the same way as a normal address.
08

Your troubleshooting toolkit

Type these into the Windows Command Prompt (click Start, type cmd, press Enter). These few cover most situations:

CommandIn plain words
ipconfig /allShows the device's full network info — its IP, gateway, DNS, and MAC. Your first look. (Real IP, or a 169.254?)
ipconfig /release then /renewThrow away the current address and ask DHCP for a fresh one. Fixes a lot of "no address" problems.
ipconfig /flushdnsClears the saved name→number list. Use when one site won't load but others do.
ping 8.8.8.8"Can I reach the internet?" 8.8.8.8 is Google's public address. Replies = your connection works.
ping google.comSame test, but by name — so it also checks DNS.
tracert google.comShows each stop along the way, so you can see where a connection dies.
nslookup google.comAsks the phone book (DNS) to look up a name — confirms DNS is answering.
# Quick health check on a PC that "has no internet" ipconfig /all # got a real IP? or a 169.254.x.x? ping 8.8.8.8 # internet reachable by number? ping google.com # reachable by name? (tests DNS)
09

The game plan

Almost every "it's not working" is one thing failing in a chain. Check it in order, bottom to top:

1

Is it physically connected? Cable clicked in, Wi-Fi joined, link light on. Start here — it's the most common cause.

2

Does it have a real address? Run ipconfig /all. A 169.254 address means DHCP failed and the PC gave itself a useless one.

3

Can it reach the internet? ping 8.8.8.8. Replies = the connection itself is fine.

4

Do names work? ping 8.8.8.8 works but ping google.com fails → it's a DNS problem, not a connection problem.

The one rule to remember: ping by number works but ping by name fails → DNS. Neither works → check the cable and the address first with ipconfig.

11

TCP vs UDP

Both carry data; the difference is reliable vs fast.

Think of it like sending a message. TCP is registered mail — every piece is confirmed and re-sent if lost, arriving complete and in order. UDP is shouting across a room — fast, but if a word is missed, it's gone. You pick based on whether accuracy or speed matters more.

TCP — reliable

Confirms delivery, re-sends lost pieces, keeps order. Used where accuracy matters: web, email, file transfer, remote desktop.

UDP — fast

No delivery guarantee, no re-sending — just speed. Used where a little loss is fine: voice calls, video streaming, DNS lookups.

SMBWindows file & printer sharing

Ports

Each service on a device listens on a numbered port. When a firewall "blocks a port," it's blocking one specific service.

Think of it like an apartment building. The IP address gets the envelope to the right building; the port number gets it to the right apartment (service) inside. Web lives in apartment 443, email in another, and so on.

The OSI map

You won't memorize this. It's a map everyone uses to point at where a problem is — "is this a cable problem or a name problem?" It has 7 layers; always troubleshoot from the bottom up.

7ApplicationWhat the user sees — websites, email, file sharing
6PresentationFormatting & encryption — HTTPS/TLS
5SessionOpening & keeping connections between apps
4TransportReliable vs fast — TCP & UDP, ports
3NetworkAddresses & routing between networks — IP, routers
2Data LinkLocal delivery on one network — MAC, switches
1PhysicalThe actual signal — cables, Wi-Fi radio, connectors
Memory aid (top to bottom): "All People Seem To Need Data Processing." In practice: confirm the cable (layer 1) before you suspect DNS (layer 7).
14

Subnetting (the "which neighborhood" math)

This is the math behind which addresses count as "the same neighborhood." It's more advanced — here's just the gist.

An IP address splits into a network part (which neighborhood) and a host part (which house). The subnet mask draws that dividing line.

Example: 192.168.1.50 /24 — the /24 means the first three numbers (192.168.1) are the neighborhood, and the last number (50) is the specific house. Everything that shares 192.168.1.x is on the same network and can talk directly.
Two addresses in each neighborhood are reserved: the first (the "network address," e.g. 192.168.1.0) and the last (the "broadcast address," e.g. 192.168.1.255). That's why a /24 has 256 total but only 254 usable for devices.

16

VPN & remote access

A VPN (Virtual Private Network) lets a device somewhere else behave as if it's safely inside the office network.

Think of it like a private, armored tunnel through the public internet. A staff member at home connects through the tunnel, and everything they send is scrambled (encrypted) so nobody along the way can read it.

Remote-access VPN

One person (e.g. working from home) connects securely back to the office network.

Site-to-site VPN

Permanently links two office locations' networks together over the internet.

Why it matters here: a VPN protects patient information while it travels — a key safeguard when staff access systems from outside the building under HIPAA.
17

Firewalls

A firewall decides what traffic is allowed in and out, using an ordered rulebook. Each rule says allow or deny certain traffic. Rules are read top to bottom and stop at the first match — so order matters.

Think of it like a security guard with a checklist. Each visitor (packet) is checked against the list from the top; the first rule that matches decides whether they're let in. Anything not on the "allow" list is turned away by default.

Default deny

Allow only what's needed; block everything else. The secure starting stance.

Stateful

Remembers connections it has seen, so when your PC asks for a page, the reply is allowed back automatically.

Inbound vs outbound

Inbound blocks unwanted traffic from outside; outbound controls what your machines may send (stops malware "phoning home").

Host vs network

A network firewall protects everything behind it; a host firewall (e.g. Windows Defender Firewall) runs on one machine.

Why it matters here: firewall rules plus network segmentation (next section) show that systems holding patient data are shielded from the open internet — part of meeting HIPAA security expectations.
18

VLANs & network design

A VLAN splits one physical switch into several separate networks. Each switch port is tagged with a VLAN, and devices only see traffic within their own VLAN — even though they share the same hardware.

Think of it like floors in one building. The Guest floor and the Clinical floor share the same building (switch), but people on one floor can't wander onto another. They only connect where a router — with firewall rules — lets them.
By design, VLANs can't talk to each other through the switch alone. Allowing specific cross-VLAN traffic has to go through a router, and that's exactly where firewall rules decide which flows are permitted.

A sensible layout for a clinic

Clinical / EHR Staff Guest Wi-Fi VoIP phones Printers / devices

Keep everything separate by default, then allow only the necessary paths — e.g. Staff may reach one specific server, while Guest reaches nothing internal at all.
